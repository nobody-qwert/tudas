# A Generic Systems Model for Humans as Open Living Systems

## 1. Purpose

This model describes a human being as an **open, dynamic, adaptive system** embedded in physical, biological, and informational environments.

The goal is not to reduce a human to only physical flows, but to create a general systems framework that can account for:

- matter entering and leaving the body,
- energy entering, being transformed, stored, and leaving,
- information entering, being processed, stored, and expressed,
- microorganisms and other organisms living on or within the body,
- external organisms that may be beneficial, neutral, competitive, parasitic, or pathogenic,
- internal regulation and feedback,
- changes of state through time,
- interactions between the human and the surrounding environment.

At the most generic level:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Matter} + \text{Energy} + \text{Information} + \text{Living Agents}
\rightarrow
\text{Human System}
\rightarrow
\text{Matter} + \text{Energy} + \text{Information} + \text{Living Agents}
}
\]

This is best treated as a **network of interacting subsystems**, rather than as a simple input-output machine.

---

## 2. The Human as an Open System

A human continuously exchanges material, energy, information, and organisms with the environment.

A useful first abstraction is:

```text
                 ENVIRONMENT
                      │
        ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
        │             │             │
      Matter        Energy      Information
        │             │             │
        └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                      │
               ┌─────────────┐
               │   HUMAN     │
               │   SYSTEM    │
               └─────────────┘
                      │
        ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
        │             │             │
      Matter        Energy      Information
        │             │             │
        └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                      │
                 ENVIRONMENT
```

This simple diagram is useful, but incomplete because the human is also inhabited by and interacting with many living organisms.

A more complete model adds a biological layer.

## 3. Four Fundamental Classes of Interaction

### 3.1 Matter

Matter includes substances that enter, leave, accumulate in, or are transformed by the system.

### Matter inputs

Examples include:

- water,
- carbohydrates,
- fats,
- proteins,
- amino acids,
- vitamins,
- minerals,
- salts and electrolytes,
- oxygen,
- medicines,
- environmental chemicals,
- microorganisms,
- particles and pollutants.

Food is important because it carries both **matter** and **chemical energy**.

### Matter outputs

Examples include:

- carbon dioxide,
- water vapor,
- sweat,
- urine,
- feces,
- shed skin,
- hair,
- mucus,
- saliva,
- secretions,
- metabolic waste products,
- cells,
- microorganisms.

Matter may also be temporarily stored as:

- body fat,
- glycogen,
- body water,
- proteins,
- minerals,
- cellular structures.

---

## 4. Energy

The human body exchanges and transforms energy continuously.

### 4.1 Chemical energy

The main usable energy input is the chemical energy contained in food.

A simplified metabolic representation is:

\[
\text{Food} + O_2
\rightarrow
CO_2 + H_2O + \text{metabolic products} + \text{heat} + \text{work}
\]

Chemical energy may be:

- immediately consumed,
- converted into ATP and other biochemical energy carriers,
- stored as fat,
- stored as glycogen,
- used in biosynthesis,
- converted into heat,
- converted into mechanical work,
- used to maintain electrochemical gradients.

### 4.2 Thermal energy

Heat can flow:

- from the body to the environment,
- from the environment to the body.

Heat transfer occurs through:

- conduction,
- convection,
- radiation,
- evaporation.

Humans normally generate substantial internal heat through metabolism.

### 4.3 Mechanical energy

Mechanical energy may enter through:

- impacts,
- pressure,
- vibration,
- acceleration,
- movement of the body by external forces,
- massage,
- physical contact,
- sound waves.

Mechanical energy may leave through:

- movement,
- locomotion,
- lifting,
- force generation,
- pressure,
- vibration,
- speech,
- sound production.

### 4.4 Electromagnetic energy

Examples include:

- visible light,
- infrared radiation,
- ultraviolet radiation,
- radio-frequency energy,
- other electromagnetic radiation.

Some forms mainly provide information, some produce heat, some may alter biological tissue.

### 4.5 Electrical energy

Electrical interactions include:

- endogenous electrical activity in nerves and muscles,
- externally applied electrical stimulation,
- electrical shocks,
- interaction with electric fields.

The body internally converts chemical energy into electrochemical gradients and electrical signaling.

---

## 5. Information

Information deserves its own category even though information must be carried by physical processes.

The **energy contained in a signal** and the **information represented by that signal** are not the same thing.

For example, a sound wave carries mechanical energy, but its pattern may also encode:

- language,
- music,
- warnings,
- emotional signals,
- social meaning.

Thus the same physical input may be both:

\[
\text{Energy carrier} + \text{Information content}
\]

### 5.1 Sensory information inputs

Examples include:

- light and visual patterns,
- sound,
- speech,
- music,
- vibration,
- touch,
- temperature,
- pain,
- pressure,
- smell,
- taste,
- proprioceptive signals,
- vestibular signals.

### 5.2 Social and symbolic information

Examples include:

- language,
- written text,
- symbols,
- cultural signals,
- education,
- news,
- interpersonal communication,
- social approval or rejection,
- instructions,
- threats,
- promises.

### 5.3 Internal information

The human system also produces, stores, and processes internal information through:

- neural activity,
- hormonal signaling,
- immune signaling,
- genetic information,
- epigenetic regulation,
- cellular signaling,
- memory,
- learned models of the environment.

### 5.4 Information outputs

Examples include:

- speech,
- facial expressions,
- posture,
- gestures,
- movement,
- writing,
- digital communication,
- emotional signals,
- decisions,
- actions that alter the environment.

---

## 6. Signals Can Have Small Energy but Large Effects

A critical systems principle is that the **physical energy of an input does not determine the magnitude of its effect**.

For example:

- a spoken sentence contains very little energy,
- but it may significantly alter behavior,
- stress state,
- mood,
- beliefs,
- memory,
- hormonal activity,
- social relationships,
- future actions.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Effect size} \neq \text{Input energy magnitude}
\]

The informational meaning of a signal can cause the human system to release and redirect much larger amounts of internally stored energy.

This is a general systems phenomenon similar to a small control signal switching a much larger process.

---

## 7. The Human as a Biological Ecosystem

The human body is not biologically isolated.

It is inhabited by communities of:

- bacteria,
- archaea,
- fungi,
- viruses,
- bacteriophages,
- and other microorganisms.

These organisms occupy different ecological niches, including:

- gastrointestinal tract,
- skin,
- mouth,
- respiratory surfaces,
- urogenital tract.

The human host plus its associated microbial communities can be modeled as a **composite biological system**.

One useful term is **holobiont**, meaning the host organism together with its associated microorganisms.

---

## 8. Microbiome as a Functional Subsystem

Microorganisms living on and within the body perform many functions.

Depending on location and species, they may contribute to:

- digestion,
- fermentation,
- production of metabolites,
- synthesis or availability of certain vitamins,
- metabolism of dietary compounds,
- maintenance of local chemical conditions,
- competition with pathogens,
- immune system development,
- immune regulation,
- maintenance of epithelial barriers,
- signaling between organs.

Examples include microbial contributions to:

- vitamin K metabolism,
- some B-vitamin pathways,
- short-chain fatty acid production,
- maintenance of acidic environments,
- colonization resistance against pathogens.

---

## 9. Surface Protection and Microbial Ecology

Skin and mucosal surfaces are ecosystems.

Resident microbes can help defend these surfaces by:

- occupying physical niches,
- consuming available nutrients,
- producing inhibitory metabolites,
- modifying pH,
- producing antimicrobial substances,
- interacting with immune cells,
- preventing easier colonization by competing organisms.

A well-known example is the role of **Lactobacillus** species in helping maintain an acidic environment in the healthy vaginal microbiome.

Other body surfaces contain different characteristic microbial communities.

The important systems idea is not that every microorganism is always beneficial, but that:

\[
\text{Health state}
=
f(\text{host},\ \text{microbial community},\ \text{environment},\ \text{time})
\]

---

## 10. Organisms Are Not Merely Matter

Living organisms entering or leaving the human system could technically be counted as matter.

However, for systems modeling they deserve their own class because they possess autonomous behavior.

Living agents can:

- metabolize,
- reproduce,
- mutate,
- adapt,
- migrate,
- cooperate,
- compete,
- communicate,
- attack,
- defend,
- modify their environment.

Therefore it is often useful to distinguish:

\[
\boxed{\text{Matter}}
\]

from

\[
\boxed{\text{Living Agents}}
\]

even though all living agents are physically made of matter.

---

## 11. Internal and External Living Agents

### 11.1 Internal or associated organisms

These include organisms:

- living inside the body,
- attached to body surfaces,
- transiently passing through the body.

Their relationship with the host may be:

- beneficial,
- commensal,
- neutral,
- opportunistic,
- parasitic,
- pathogenic.

### 11.2 External organisms

The human also interacts continuously with organisms outside the body.

Examples include:

- other humans,
- domestic animals,
- wild animals,
- plants,
- insects,
- fungi,
- parasites,
- pathogens,
- food organisms,
- environmental microbial communities.

These may act as:

- allies,
- symbionts,
- competitors,
- predators,
- parasites,
- pathogens,
- food sources,
- information sources,
- social partners.

The classification is contextual rather than permanent.

The same organism can be harmless under one condition and harmful under another.

## 12. Nested System Boundaries

A single system boundary is not always sufficient.

The model can use multiple nested boundaries.

```text
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│            PHYSICAL ENVIRONMENT             │
│                                             │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │       BIOLOGICAL ENVIRONMENT        │   │
│   │                                     │   │
│   │   ┌─────────────────────────────┐   │   │
│   │   │   HUMAN + MICROBIOME        │   │   │
│   │   │                             │   │   │
│   │   │   ┌─────────────────────┐   │   │   │
│   │   │   │ HUMAN HOST CELLS    │   │   │   │
│   │   │   └─────────────────────┘   │   │   │
│   │   │                             │   │   │
│   │   └─────────────────────────────┘   │   │
│   │                                     │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Different questions require different system boundaries.

For example:

- physiology may focus on the human host,
- microbiome research may use the host-plus-microbiome boundary,
- epidemiology may use groups of humans and pathogens,
- ecology may include humans, other species, and the environment.

## 13. Internal Human Subsystems

The human itself can be decomposed into interacting subsystems.

Examples include:

- nervous system,
- endocrine system,
- immune system,
- cardiovascular system,
- respiratory system,
- digestive system,
- renal system,
- musculoskeletal system,
- reproductive system,
- integumentary system,
- microbiome,
- metabolic networks.

These systems exchange:

- matter,
- energy,
- information,
- signaling molecules,
- cells,
- mechanical forces.

No subsystem operates independently.

---

## 14. Internal State

An input-output model is incomplete without describing **state**.

The output of a human system depends not only on present inputs but also on the system's current internal condition.

A generic representation is:

\[
X_{t+1} = F(X_t, U_t, E_t)
\]

where:

- \(X_t\) = current internal state,
- \(U_t\) = current inputs,
- \(E_t\) = environmental conditions,
- \(F\) = system dynamics.

Possible state variables include:

- body temperature,
- hydration,
- nutrient reserves,
- blood glucose,
- hormonal state,
- immune state,
- microbiome composition,
- neural activity,
- emotional state,
- memory,
- fatigue,
- tissue condition.

The same input can therefore produce different outputs in different states.

---

## 15. Feedback

Living systems are dominated by feedback.

### Negative feedback

Negative feedback tends to stabilize variables.

Examples:

- thermoregulation,
- blood glucose regulation,
- blood pressure regulation,
- acid-base regulation.

Generic form:

```text
Disturbance
    │
    v
Measured variable
    │
    v
Controller
    │
    v
Corrective response
    │
    └──────────> reduces disturbance
```

### Positive feedback

Positive feedback amplifies changes.

Examples may include:

- blood clotting cascades,
- action potential depolarization phases,
- some inflammatory responses,
- labor contractions.

Positive feedback is usually constrained by other mechanisms.

---

## 16. Adaptive Feedback

Humans also modify future responses based on experience.

This includes:

- learning,
- memory,
- immune memory,
- physiological adaptation,
- behavioral adaptation,
- microbial ecosystem changes.

This means that the system itself changes as it operates.

A more realistic model is therefore:

\[
\text{Input}
\rightarrow
\text{Processing}
\rightarrow
\text{Output}
\rightarrow
\text{Environmental change}
\rightarrow
\text{New input}
\]

while simultaneously:

\[
\text{Processing}
\rightarrow
\text{Modification of future processing}
\]

---

## 17. Time Scales

Human-system processes occur on very different time scales.

Examples:

| Time Scale | Example Processes |
|---|---|
| milliseconds | neural signaling |
| seconds | muscular response, breathing changes |
| minutes | hormonal and metabolic adjustments |
| hours | digestion, hydration shifts |
| days | immune responses, sleep cycles |
| weeks | training adaptation, microbiome changes |
| months | tissue remodeling |
| years | development, aging, long-term learning |
| generations | genetic evolution |

A systems model should therefore specify its time resolution.

---

## 18. Storage and Buffers

The system contains internal reservoirs that decouple input from output.

Examples include:

- fat as energy storage,
- glycogen,
- body water,
- mineral stores,
- oxygen bound to hemoglobin,
- nutrient pools,
- memory,
- immune memory.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Input at time } t
\]

does not necessarily produce

\[
\text{Output at time } t
\]

Storage introduces delay and history dependence.

## 22. A Generic Mathematical Systems Form

A reusable abstract model can be written as:

\[
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
=
F(
\mathbf{x}_t,
\mathbf{m}_t,
\mathbf{e}_t,
\mathbf{i}_t,
\mathbf{l}_t,
\mathbf{p}_t
)
\]

where:

- \(\mathbf{x}_t\) = internal system state,
- \(\mathbf{m}_t\) = matter flows,
- \(\mathbf{e}_t\) = energy flows,
- \(\mathbf{i}_t\) = information flows,
- \(\mathbf{l}_t\) = living-agent interactions,
- \(\mathbf{p}_t\) = environmental parameters.

Outputs can be represented as:

\[
\mathbf{y}_t = G(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{u}_t)
\]

with:

\[
\mathbf{u}_t =
[
\mathbf{m}_t,
\mathbf{e}_t,
\mathbf{i}_t,
\mathbf{l}_t,
\mathbf{p}_t
]
\]

This makes the model suitable for simulation, systems biology, control theory, ecology, or computational modeling.

## 23. A Reusable Generic Systems Template

The same framework can be used beyond humans.

For any system, identify:

### A. System boundary

What is inside the model and what is outside?

### B. Inputs

Classify them as:

1. Matter
2. Energy
3. Information
4. Living or autonomous agents

### C. Outputs

Classify them using the same categories.

### D. Internal state

What variables describe the present condition of the system?

### E. Storage

What can accumulate inside the system?

### F. Transformations

How are inputs converted into outputs or stored states?

### G. Subsystems

What internal components interact?

### H. Feedback

What outputs influence future inputs or internal behavior?

### I. Environment

What external conditions modify system behavior?

### J. Time scale

How quickly do the important processes change?

### K. Adaptation

Does the system modify its own future behavior?

### L. Other agents

Are there other living or autonomous entities interacting with the system?

The complete framework can be compressed to:

\[
\boxed{

\leftrightarrow
\text{System}
}
\]

where the system contains:

\[
\boxed{
\text{State}
+
\text{Storage}

\text{Transformations}
+
\text{Subsystems}
+
\text{Feedback}
+
\text{Adaptation}
}
\]

and changes through time:

\[
\boxed{
X_{t+1}=F(X_t,\text{inputs},\text{environment})
}
\]

## 25. Main Conceptual Conclusion

A human is better modeled not as an isolated machine, but as a **nested, open, adaptive ecosystem**.

It continuously exchanges:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Matter}
+
\text{Energy}
+
\text{Information}
+
\text{Living Agents}
}
\]

with its surroundings.

Its behavior depends on:

- current inputs,
- internal state,
- stored resources,
- microbiome and associated organisms,
- external organisms,
- physical conditions,
- previous history,
- feedback,
- learning and adaptation,
- the time scale being studied.

This framework is broad enough to serve as a starting point for more detailed models in physiology, medicine, ecology, control theory, systems biology, robotics, artificial systems, or general systems science.
